2. Forecasting Assumptions & Methodology
PDF page 4

This is the foundation of the financial model. It explains how historical figures are converted into projected financial statements for FY2027–FY2031.
2.1 Income statement assumptions
The report uses the following forecasting logic:
Item	Methodology used in the PDF
Revenue	Previous year's revenue × (1 + growth rate)
Cost of goods sold (COGS)	Average historical COGS margin × forecast revenue
Selling and other expenses	Average historical expense-to-revenue ratio × forecast revenue
Depreciation	Average historical depreciation-to-revenue ratio × forecast revenue
Interest	Forecast borrowings × 4.54%
Effective tax rate	Average historical effective tax rate

Explanation: Instead of estimating every expense independently, the model links many costs to revenue using historical percentages. This makes the forecast easier to update when revenue growth assumptions change.

For example, if forecast revenue increases and the COGS margin remains constant, the model automatically increases forecast COGS. The same logic applies to selling expenses and depreciation.
2.2 Equity and profit retention
The report uses these assumptions:
- Equity shares: Forecast shares are based on the last historical year's closing share count.
- Dividend paid: Dividend per share (DPS) multiplied by the number of equity shares.
- Retained earnings: Net profit less dividends.
- Equity share capital: Carried forward from the previous year.
- Closing reserves: Beginning reserves plus net profit less dividends.
These calculations link the income statement to shareholders' equity on the balance sheet.
2.3 Balance-sheet assumptions
The report models the main balance-sheet items as follows:
- Borrowings: Maintained at the last historical year's level.
- Other liabilities: Forecast using the historical other-liabilities-to-revenue ratio.
- Net block / property, plant and equipment (PP&E): Beginning net block plus capital expenditure minus depreciation.
- Capital work in progress (CWIP): Forecast using its historical relationship with revenue.
- Investments: Forecast using the historical investment-to-revenue ratio.
- Other assets: Forecast using the historical other-assets-to-revenue ratio.
These assumptions determine how the company's asset base, liabilities, and equity develop over the forecast period.
2.4 Working-capital assumptions
Working capital represents funds tied up in day-to-day operations.

The model calculates:
- Days sales outstanding (DSO): Receivables ÷ revenue × 365.
- Receivables forecast: Average historical DSO × forecast revenue ÷ 365.
- Inventory days: Inventory ÷ COGS × 365.
- Inventory forecast: Average historical inventory days × forecast COGS ÷ 365.
- Other working capital: Change in other liabilities less change in other assets.
- Total working-capital change: Receivables change plus inventory change plus other working-capital changes.
An increase in receivables typically consumes cash because revenue has been recognised but the customer has not yet paid. A decrease in receivables typically releases cash.
2.5 Cash-flow assumptions
The report sets out the following relationships:
- Cash flow from operations (CFO): Net profit plus depreciation and working-capital changes.
- Cash flow from investing (CFI): Capital expenditure, changes in CWIP, and other investing items.
- Cash flow from financing (CFF): Dividends and debt movements.
- Net change in cash: CFO + CFI + CFF.
- Ending cash: Beginning cash + net change in cash.
2.6 Balance-sheet integrity
The model includes a balance-sheet check so that:
\[
\text{Total Assets}=\text{Total Liabilities and Equity}
\]

The PDF reports the check as TRUE across the historical and forecast periods. It also explicitly states that cash is derived from the cash-flow statement and linked to the balance sheet rather than being used as a balancing plug.

Why this matters: A model that balances because cash is artificially forced to a balancing figure may hide errors. Linking cash to cash flows creates a more meaningful check of the model's internal consistency. Infosys_Full_Model_Professional(2).pdf

The formula is:
\[

13.2 The three-factor DuPont equation
The report uses the following decomposition:
\[
ROE=A\times B\times C
\]

where:
- A — Net profit margin: Net profit ÷ revenue.
- B — Asset turnover: Revenue ÷ average total assets.
- C — Equity multiplier: Average total assets ÷ average shareholder equity.
This equation helps explain whether ROE is being driven by profit margins, how efficiently assets generate sales, or the amount of assets supported by each rupee of equity.
